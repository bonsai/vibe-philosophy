# Platonic Modeling

## Purpose
Use ideal forms and constructed models as tools for asking what a concept aims to capture, while keeping the model distinct from real cases.

## Procedure
1. State the question and the concept being modeled.
2. Describe the ideal, standard, or theoretical model being proposed.
3. Identify the model's parts, roles, hierarchy, and assumptions.
4. Explain how knowledge, education, authority, and governance relate within the model, where relevant.
5. Compare the model with historical or observed cases.
6. Identify omissions, counterexamples, and rival interpretations.
7. State what the model explains and what it cannot establish.

## Historical care
When discussing Plato's *Republic*, distinguish its theoretical city and commonly described roles—rulers/guardians, auxiliaries, and producers—from actual historical institutions and legal categories such as citizen and enslaved person. They are not interchangeable classifications.

## Output
- Ideal/model
- Components and relationships
- Assumptions and intended purpose
- Differences from observed reality
- Alternative interpretations and limitations

## Boundaries
- An ideal model is not a description of every real society.
- Internal consistency does not establish historical truth or moral legitimacy.
- Do not present contested readings of Plato as settled without evidence.

## Verification
For every major conclusion, identify whether it follows from the model's assumptions or from independent historical evidence.
