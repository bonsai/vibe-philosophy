# Civic Status Boundaries

## Purpose
Analyze who counts as a citizen, who is enslaved or free, and who can participate in a specified political function, using historically grounded definitions.

## Required scope
For every analysis, identify:
1. Place and period.
2. Primary source, law, inscription, or relevant scholarship.
3. Dimension: legal status, political participation, movement, property, labor, kinship, or social recognition.
4. Authority that assigns or enforces the status.
5. Exceptions and people whose status does not fit the main categories.
6. Whether the question is descriptive, interpretive, or ethical.

## Procedure
1. Define each status independently.
2. Establish the population under study and the source's terminology.
3. Distinguish citizenship, freedom, enslavement, residence, and political participation.
4. Record overlaps, exclusions, exceptions, and disputed cases.
5. Compare the written rule with evidence of actual practice when available.
6. State what the evidence cannot establish.

## Core guardrail
Never infer “not a citizen” means “enslaved.” A person may be free but lack citizenship; categories are not exhaustive complements in every society.

## Output
- Scope and source
- Status definitions
- Inclusion/exclusion criteria
- Exceptional or uncertain cases
- Descriptive findings
- Ethical assessment, if requested, clearly separated

## Verification
Every status claim must be traceable to a specified source and dimension. Mark undocumented classifications as uncertain rather than filling gaps by inference.
