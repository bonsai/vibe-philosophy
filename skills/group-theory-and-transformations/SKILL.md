# Group Theory and Transformations

## Purpose
Determine whether a system's transformations form a group, and use invariants to describe what changes and what stays the same.

## Procedure
1. Specify the objects being transformed.
2. Define the allowed transformations and their composition.
3. Check closure under composition.
4. Check associativity, an identity transformation, and an inverse for every transformation.
5. State the properties or relations preserved by the transformations.
6. If any axiom fails, choose a better model such as a directed graph, state-transition system, monoid, category, or relation.
7. Interpret the result only within the defined model.

## Common uses
- Permutations of a finite set.
- Symmetries of a diagram or structure.
- Relabelings that preserve specified relationships.
- Invariants under a defined transformation family.

## Caution
Do not assume that historical status changes—such as enslavement, emancipation, citizenship acquisition, or disenfranchisement—form a group. Such changes may be constrained, directed, and non-reversible. A state-transition graph may fit better.

A mathematical symmetry does not imply equality, justice, or symmetry in the real institution. Name exactly what is preserved.

## Output
- Objects and transformations
- Composition rule
- Axiom checks
- Invariants
- Model chosen and why
- Interpretive limits

## Verification
Provide a concrete check or counterexample for each group axiom. If an axiom fails, do not call the structure a group.
